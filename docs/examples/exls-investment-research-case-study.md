# EXLS investment-research case study

> **Status:** Illustrative example; non-normative
>
> **Purpose:** Demonstrate how the Investment Research Vision could be applied to
> one IT Services/BPO company.
>
> **Important:** All figures, dates, hypotheses, option terms, and conclusions
> below are hypothetical teaching inputs unless independently linked to a
> canonical source. They are not current ValuePilot facts, investment advice, an
> architecture contract, or acceptance evidence.

The original research compendium used EXLS repeatedly to illustrate persistent
thesis memory. This case study consolidates those fragments so the Vision does
not confuse examples with product requirements.

## 1. Why EXLS is a useful first vertical slice

EXLS can exercise several research questions in one company:

- an understandable services business with measurable operating history;
- possible workflow switching costs and vertical specialization;
- organic versus acquisition growth;
- AI/data growth claims;
- employee productivity and margin questions;
- stock-based compensation and buybacks;
- leverage and capital allocation;
- competitor comparison;
- valuation and optional Put underwriting.

It is only a first vertical slice. It does not prove that the architecture works
for banks, insurers, REITs, cyclical industrials, semiconductors, ADR/FX cases,
restatements, negative-FCF growth companies, or acquisition-heavy disclosures.

## 2. Illustrative initial research view

### Business model questions

```text
What does EXLS sell?
Who buys it and why?
How deeply is it integrated into customer workflows?
What portion of growth is organic?
Which services have attractive incremental economics?
How much value from AI productivity is retained by EXLS?
```

### Positive hypotheses

```text
H1 — Workflow integration creates meaningful switching costs.

H2 — Vertical expertise in insurance, healthcare, and banking creates
     a durable competitive advantage.

H3 — Data and AI capabilities expand wallet share inside existing customers.

H4 — A meaningful portion of AI productivity gains can be retained rather
     than passed entirely to customers.

H5 — Organic revenue can compound at a double-digit rate over a five-year
     horizon without materially reducing incremental returns.
```

### Risk and alternative hypotheses

```text
R1 — Customers capture most AI productivity benefits through lower pricing.

R2 — Growth advantage reflects smaller scale or a temporary demand phase.

R3 — Acquisitions inflate reported growth and reduce economic returns.

R4 — Competitors weaken pricing or wallet-share growth.

R5 — Stock-based compensation materially dilutes owner economics.

R6 — Buybacks or acquisitions increase leverage without creating per-share
     value.

R7 — Growth slows as the company reaches a larger revenue base.
```

### Known unknowns

- How much AI-driven productivity is measurable in revenue per employee?
- How much benefit is retained versus passed to customers?
- Is wallet-share expansion disclosed or independently observable?
- Are switching costs increasing, stable, or merely assumed?
- How does growth compare with economically relevant peers on a like-for-like
  organic basis?

## 3. Evidence map

An illustrative evidence relationship could look like:

```text
Reported organic growth for a named quarter
        -> evidence for H5
        -> not proof of a five-year growth claim

Customer expansion disclosed by the company
        -> possible evidence for H1/H3
        -> remains a management claim until corroborated

Revenue growth exceeding employee growth
        -> possible productivity evidence for H4
        -> alternative explanations include price, mix, outsourcing, FX,
           acquisitions, or timing

Higher leverage and large buybacks
        -> evidence relevant to R5/R6
        -> requires per-share and point-in-time valuation analysis
```

Multiple articles repeating one company announcement attach to one underlying
event. A later corrected filing changes current authority without rewriting what
was known when an earlier decision was made.

## 4. Falsification and watch conditions

Illustrative watch conditions:

- stock-based compensation rises relative to revenue and owner earnings;
- leverage rises without a clear high-return use of capital;
- customer concentration increases;
- growth decelerates faster than peers or prior expectations;
- revenue per employee does not improve despite strong AI-productivity claims.

Illustrative falsification or kill conditions:

- repeated major customer losses contradict durable switching costs;
- organic growth remains below a precommitted threshold for the defined review
  horizon without evidence that preserves the long-term mechanism;
- accounting or management credibility is impaired;
- incremental returns deteriorate persistently;
- balance-sheet risk threatens the ability to survive a downturn;
- evidence shows the central AI economics accrue primarily to customers and
  invalidate the accepted valuation assumptions.

These conditions are examples. Real thresholds require dated user acceptance,
source-backed definitions, and explicit resolution rules.

## 5. Competitive and causal questions

Potential peers might include Genpact, Accenture, Cognizant, or other companies
with sufficiently similar economics. Comparison would first normalize business
mix rather than assume every reported growth rate is comparable.

```text
Observed: EXLS grows faster than a selected peer set.

Explanation A: superior vertical specialization and wallet-share gains.
Explanation B: smaller scale.
Explanation C: different end-market or service mix.
Explanation D: acquisition contribution.
Explanation E: temporary demand or pricing.
Explanation F: competitor weakness rather than a widening EXLS moat.
```

Research seeks evidence that distinguishes these explanations. Faster growth by
itself does not prove share gain or moat expansion.

## 6. Illustrative valuation scenarios

The following values are deliberately hypothetical:

```text
Bear intrinsic value:  $38
Base intrinsic value:  $44
Bull intrinsic value:  $51
Illustrative MOS price: $33
```

The range might reflect different assumptions for normalized Owner Earnings,
organic growth, margins, competitive-advantage period, dilution, capital
requirements, and balance-sheet risk.

A decision view would not show only “MOS = 24%.” It could show:

```text
Price date:       YYYY-MM-DD
Price currency:   USD
Current price:    $XX
Base-case MOS:    calculated only from an accepted published base value
Bear-case MOS:    separate downside comparison
Source freshness: visible
```

A reverse DCF could ask what growth and margin duration the market price already
requires, then compare those expectations with the accepted research view.

No figure in this case study is a published `val.fair_value` fact.

## 7. Illustrative thesis evolution

### Initial view

```text
H1/H2: plausible but not yet strongly confirmed
H3: promising growth mechanism
H4: material unresolved question
H5: explicit long-term growth hypothesis
Valuation: hypothetical bear/base/bull range
Decision: research or wait
```

### Later supportive quarter

```text
New observations:
- organic growth above the accepted expectation range;
- guidance raised;
- slight revenue-per-employee improvement;
- customer concentration stable;
- SBC still elevated.

Proposed delta:
- H5 receives stronger supporting evidence;
- H4 receives limited support but remains unresolved;
- dilution risk remains open;
- valuation change, if any, is attributed to explicit assumptions.
```

### Later contradictory quarter

```text
New observations:
- organic growth below the accepted threshold;
- loss of a large customer;
- pricing pressure;
- flat revenue per employee.

Proposed delta:
- H1 and H4 weaken;
- H5 approaches its falsification condition;
- prior valuation requires human review;
- the case may carry a thesis-conflict risk action.
```

In each step, AI may prepare the comparison and skeptical alternatives. The
accepted research revision changes only through an explicit user action.

## 8. Illustrative option-underwriting branch

Only after an accepted fundamental view exists might an investor review a Put.
Hypothetical inputs:

```text
Strike:                     $32.50
Premium:                    $1.10 per share
Gross assignment comparison:$31.40 per share
Hypothetical base value:    $44
Hypothetical MOS price:     $33
```

The gross comparison is not a complete net economic cost. Review would also
consider expiry, quote time, spread, liquidity, contract deliverable, collateral
basis, fees, taxes, early assignment, event dates, simultaneous assignment, and
portfolio concentration.

The valid question is not “Is the premium high?” It is:

> Would the investor willingly own the business at the complete assignment
> economics under the accepted downside thesis?

The output is analysis for human review. It does not create or route an order.

## 9. Illustrative decision record

```text
Company and listing identity as displayed at decision time
Decision date and research revision
Decision: WATCH / PASS / OWN / OPTION-UNDERWRITING REVIEW
Accepted business thesis
Strongest bull and bear cases
Key supporting and contradicting evidence
Known unknowns
Falsification conditions
Valuation range, assumptions, currency, and as-of date
Current source and price freshness
Human decision and reason
Next review date where applicable
```

The record describes what the user knew and believed then. Later facts may be
shown in postmortem, but they do not rewrite the historical record.

## 10. What this case can and cannot validate

The EXLS slice can explore:

- one user-owned thesis and revision history;
- evidence mapping and alternative explanations;
- quarterly change detection;
- deterministic financial comparisons;
- valuation proposal and explicit publication;
- skeptical review;
- optional underwriting analysis;
- human decision history.

Architecture acceptance still requires representative fixtures across business
models, ownership boundaries, missing data, source corrections, point-in-time
reconstruction, multi-currency/listing identity, and concurrency. Success with
EXLS is evidence of one useful workflow, not proof of universal generalization.
